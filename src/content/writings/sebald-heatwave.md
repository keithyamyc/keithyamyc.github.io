There is a correct season for every book, and it is usually the season the author was writing in. Sebald wrote *The Rings of Saturn* in the flat grey light of East Anglia, and it is a book that expects drizzle.

I read it in August, in a flat with a broken air-conditioner, at thirty-five degrees. This was not deliberate. It was the only thing on the shelf I had not read.

## The book as weather

The novel — if it is a novel — is a walking narrative along the Suffolk coast, interrupted by digressions into silk production, the history of the herring, Chinese emperors, and the destruction of Norwich. Nothing much happens. Everything accumulates.

In grey weather, this reads as melancholy. In heat, it reads as something closer to fever. The digressions stopped feeling like erudition and started feeling like the mind's attempt to avoid a subject it cannot name.

> Sebald's sentences are long because grief is long. There is no short way to say that a world has gone.

## What the heat revealed

Two things I would have missed in November.

**First, the comedy.** There is a lot of it, and it is very dry. The narrator's attempts to find a decent meal in East Anglia are quietly hilarious. In cold weather I would have read these passages as sad; in heat, I read them as jokes, which I think is correct.

**Second, the hostility to comfort.** The book refuses to let you settle. Every time a narrative begins to feel cosy, it is interrupted by an account of destruction — a fire, a bombardment, a species extinct. Reading it in physical discomfort made the structural discomfort visible.

## Why I keep returning

I have read this book three times. Each time it has been about something different: the first time, about memory; the second, about Europe; this time, about the impossibility of finishing anything.

That is not a property of the book alone. It is what happens when you reread at intervals long enough for your own life to change the text.

## A note on the translation

Michael Hulse's English is, by common consent, a collaboration rather than a translation. Sebald's German is more clipped; Hulse's prose is slower and more ornate. Reading the German alongside — which I did for two chapters, badly — suggests that Hulse has not so much translated the sentences as translated the *atmosphere*.

I am told this is controversial among scholars. It is not controversial among readers, who mostly just want the book to keep doing what it does.

## Coda

The heatwave broke on the last day. I finished the book in the evening, with the windows open and the first rain coming in.

It was a better book in the heat. I would not recommend the method, but I would recommend the book.
