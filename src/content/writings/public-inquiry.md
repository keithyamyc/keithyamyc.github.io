There is a particular sentence that appears in almost every press release announcing a new public inquiry. It says that the exercise will be "independent, thorough, and transparent." It is a sentence written by someone who already knows what the report will say.[^1]

I have spent enough time in rooms where these documents are drafted to recognise the grammar. Independence is asserted rather than structured. Thoroughness is measured in the number of submissions received, not in the discomfort of the conclusions. Transparency, most tellingly, is now understood as a website with a timeline on it.

## The inquiry as a civic form

The public inquiry is one of the few remaining institutions in which a society is permitted to argue with itself slowly. It is inefficient by design. It takes evidence under oath, it allows the aggrieved to be heard in the same room as the powerful, and it produces a document that outlasts the government that commissioned it.

That slowness is not a bug. A finding of fact arrived at over eighteen months carries a different kind of weight from a finding arrived at over a weekend. The former is a judgement; the latter is a position.

> An inquiry that cannot embarrass the people who appointed it is not an inquiry. It is a consultation with better stationery.

When we compress the process — when terms of reference are drafted narrowly enough to exclude the awkward question, when the timetable is set so that the report lands after the election rather than before it — we do not merely get a worse report. We teach the public that the form itself is theatre.

## Three quiet erosions

**First, the narrowing of terms of reference.** The scope of an inquiry is its constitution. A remit that asks "whether procedure was followed" rather than "whether the decision was right" has already answered the question it pretends to leave open.

**Second, the professionalisation of witness.** The people who appear before inquiries are increasingly represented, coached, and media-trained. This is not improper in itself, but it changes the character of the evidence. Spontaneity is the thing that makes testimony credible; rehearsal is the thing that makes it safe.

**Third, the absorption of findings into communications strategy.** A report is now launched rather than published. It has a press conference, a social media cut, and a set of approved lines. By the time the substance is read, the framing has already hardened.

## What a better inquiry looks like

The reforms are unglamorous and mostly procedural:

- Terms of reference drafted by the commission itself, not by the department under scrutiny
- A statutory minimum period for evidence-gathering, not subject to ministerial extension or truncation
- Publication of all evidence, including that which is inconvenient, subject only to narrowly defined redactions
- A duty on the executive to respond in writing, point by point, within a fixed period

None of this requires new institutions.[^2] It requires that we stop treating accountability as a communications problem and start treating it as a constitutional one.

The inquiry is not a machine for producing closure. It is a machine for producing a record. And a record, honestly kept, is the only thing a future generation can use to hold us to account.

[^1]: This essay draws on observations of several Hong Kong and United Kingdom inquiry processes between 2019 and 2025. Names and specific proceedings are omitted deliberately.

[^2]: For a comparative treatment, see the discussion of the Scott and Saville inquiries in the standard administrative law texts.
