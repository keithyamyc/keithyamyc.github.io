Law students are taught to read judgments for the *ratio*. Extract the principle, discard the narrative, move on. It is efficient advice, and it produces competent lawyers who cannot tell you why a particular decision feels wrong even when its logic appears sound.[^1]

A judgment is not only an argument. It is a document written by a person, in a style, with omissions that are as meaningful as its assertions.

## The atmosphere of a decision

Consider two ways of disposing of the same appeal.

The first says: *"The tribunal's finding was one that was open to it on the evidence."*

The second says: *"The tribunal heard the applicant, weighed her evidence against the documentary record, and reached a conclusion it was entitled to reach."*

Legally, these are close to identical. Rhetorically, they are worlds apart. The first treats the applicant as an abstraction. The second insists, in a single clause, that she was present — that she was heard. Neither sentence changes the outcome. Both change what the outcome means to the person receiving it.

> The ethics of a judgment live in its transitions: in the sentence where the court stops describing the facts as the parties experienced them and begins describing them as the law requires.

## Reading for silence

The most instructive passages in a judgment are frequently the ones that are absent. A court that spends four pages on a procedural point and two paragraphs on the central injustice has told you something about its priorities that no headnote will record.

When I read a decision now, I keep a second column in my notes:

1. What was argued
2. What was decided
3. What was **not** addressed
4. What language was used to make the avoidance sound natural

The fourth column is where the craft is. Phrases like "it is unnecessary to decide," "the point does not arise," and "in the circumstances" are not neutral. They are load-bearing.

## Why this matters in practice

This is not an argument for aesthetic indulgence. It has three practical consequences.

**For advocates.** A court's stylistic habits predict its receptiveness. A bench that writes in short declaratory sentences responds to structure; a bench that writes at length responds to nuance. Reading for style is a form of client service.

**For judges.** The obligation to give reasons is not satisfied by the recitation of a conclusion. Reasons must be capable of being understood by the person to whom they are addressed. A judgment that is legally unimpeachable and humanly opaque has discharged only half its duty.

**For the public.** The legitimacy of adjudication depends on the perception that the losing party was taken seriously. Style is how that perception is created or destroyed.

## A modest discipline

I keep a commonplace book of judicial sentences — not for their law, but for their conduct. Sentences that show what it looks like when a court is careful with a person's life. Sentences that show the opposite.

It is a strange hobby for a lawyer, and a necessary one. Because the law is not only applied; it is written. And what is written can be read, challenged, and — occasionally — improved.
