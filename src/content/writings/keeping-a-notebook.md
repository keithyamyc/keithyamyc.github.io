I own fourteen notebooks. Eleven of them are unfinished. Two are empty, bought in a moment of optimism at an airport. One is full, and it is the least interesting of the lot.

The full one is from 2018. Every entry has a date, a heading, and a tidy paragraph. Reading it now is like reading a slightly bored person's summary of a year that was, in fact, quite dramatic. Nothing is in there. Everything has been edited out on the way in.

## The unfinished ones

The unfinished notebooks are different. They contain:

- a train ticket from Kyoto, pressed flat, with two words written beside it: *too loud*
- the name of a pharmacist in Lisbon whose handwriting I wanted to copy
- four pages of an argument I had with someone I no longer speak to, written in a fury, and then a line in different ink: *I was wrong about the second half*
- a list of things I could hear from a hotel window in Naples at 2 a.m.
- nothing for seven months

These are not records. They are residues. And residues turn out to be far more useful than records when you are trying to remember what a year actually felt like.

## Why we stop writing in them

The standard answer is time. The real answer is that a notebook stops being useful the moment it starts being a performance. Once you notice that you are writing for a future reader — even a future version of yourself — the sentences stiffen. You begin to explain instead of notice.

> The notebook is not the place where you become a writer. It is the place where you are allowed not to be one.

The best entry in any of my notebooks is four words long. It says: *the light was strange.* I have no idea what day it was, or what I was looking at, or why it mattered. But I remember the feeling of writing it, standing at a window, and that is more than the full notebook can offer.

## A practical defence

If you want to keep a notebook and are worried about doing it badly, here is what has worked for me.

**Write for nobody.** If a sentence sounds like it could be published, cross it out. Publication is a later and separate act.

**Do not date everything.** Dates make you feel behind. Some of the best entries should be undateable, so that they float free of the year they came from.

**Leave gaps.** A notebook with empty pages is a notebook that still has a future.

**Keep the tickets.** The objects do the remembering; you only need to leave them room.

## Coda

I bought a fifteenth notebook last week. It is small, soft-covered, and already slightly warped from being in my coat pocket. I have written two lines in it. One of them is wrong.

I am looking forward to forgetting what it meant.
