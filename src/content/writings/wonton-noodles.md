There are two kinds of Hong Kong breakfast. The first is the one in the guidebooks: dim sum, white tablecloths, a trolley, a photograph of the founder on the wall. The second is the one that begins at five in the morning, standing up, for thirty-eight dollars.

I have been eating at Wah Kee for eleven years. In that time the price has risen by nine dollars, the menu has not changed, and the woman who takes the orders has not once asked me what I want.

## The bowl

![The bowl arrives at 6:12 a.m. — bamboo-pressed noodles, six wontons, a teaspoon of lard at the bottom.](/images/food/wonton-01.svg "side")

Cantonese wonton noodles are an exercise in restraint. There are four components and no more: the broth, the noodle, the wonton, and the lard.

The broth here is made from dried flounder, prawn shell, and pork bone, simmered overnight and never allowed to boil hard. It is clear, faintly sweet, and tastes of the sea rather than of seasoning. The noodles are bamboo-pressed, which gives them the particular bite — *sung* — that machine-rolled noodles cannot produce. The wontons are small, six to a bowl, with a filling of prawn and a little pork fat, wrapped so thinly that you can see the pink through the skin.

And underneath it all, a teaspoon of rendered lard. Without it the bowl is merely correct. With it, the bowl is a memory.

> The measure of a noodle shop is not the quality of its best bowl. It is the consistency of its four-hundredth.

## What to order

- **Wonton noodles, dry (撈麵)** — the default. The sauce arrives on the side of the bowl, not on the noodles, so you control the salt.
- **Extra wontons** — six is not enough at six in the morning.
- **Hot tea, no sugar** — the pot is refilled without being asked.

What not to order: the braised beef. It is fine. It is not the reason to come.

![The counter, rebuilt in 1998, unchanged since. The pot has not been off the heat in living memory.](/images/food/wonton-02.svg)

## The arithmetic

A bowl costs thirty-eight dollars. The shop opens at five and closes when the dough runs out, usually before two. There is no air conditioning to speak of, the stools are plastic, and the queue at eight in the morning runs down the block.

You do not go for the room. You go because there is a version of a city that exists only between five and seven in the morning, when the people who make it work are eating before the people who write about it have woken up.

That city is worth getting up for.
