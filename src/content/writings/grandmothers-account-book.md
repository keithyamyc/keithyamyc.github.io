My grandmother died in March. Among the things she left was a stack of exercise books, the kind with the red cover and the thin grey rule, the kind every schoolchild in Hong Kong used in the 1970s.

They are account books. Every day, from 1979 until a few weeks before her death, she wrote down what she spent.

## The form

The entries are not sentences. They are columns:

| Date | Item | Amount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3/4 | 菜 | 12 |
| 3/4 | 車 | 4.5 |
| 4/4 | 藥 | 68 |

No commentary. No totals except at the end of each month, underlined twice. The handwriting changes over four decades — rounder in the early books, then sharper, then in the last two years, unsteady and large.

## What the numbers say

You can read a life out of them if you are patient.

**1979–1985.** Small amounts, frequent. Vegetables bought daily because there was no refrigerator large enough to keep more. Bus fares recorded separately from tram fares, which tells you she was still working in Kowloon and crossing the harbour.

**1986.** A sudden column of larger entries: a hospital, a funeral, three months of expenses for two households. This is the year my grandfather died. She never wrote his name in the books. She did not need to.

**1994.** The first entry for a piano lesson. Mine. Followed by eleven years of them, every month, the same amount, never missing.

**2003.** A new category appears: *mask*. Then *alcohol*. Then, for a while, nothing much at all.

**2016 onward.** More medicine than food. Fewer bus fares. Then no bus fares.

## The absence of the self

She never wrote a word about how she felt. Not once, in forty-one years.

And yet the books are more revealing than any diary could be, because a ledger cannot lie about what a person actually did with their days. You cannot fake forty-one years of buying vegetables.

> A diary tells you what a person thought of their life. An account book tells you what their life was.

## The last page

The final entry is dated two weeks before she died. It reads:

> 3/2 餅 22

A cake. Twenty-two dollars. There is no indication of who it was for. My mother thinks it was for the nurses on the ward, because she had been bringing them things for a month. I think it might have been for herself.

Either way, it is the last thing we know for certain that she did: she bought something sweet, and she wrote it down.

## What I keep

The books are in a box under my desk. I have not decided what to do with them. Digitising them feels like a betrayal of their physicality — the smudge of oil on the 1983 pages, the place where a cup was set down.

For now I keep them, and occasionally I open one at random and read a month. It is the closest thing I have to hearing her speak.

She would have found that sentimental. She would have written down the cost of the box.
