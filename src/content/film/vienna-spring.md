Vienna is a city that has been photographed to death, and it knows it. The postcard version — the palace, the opera, the horses — is so thoroughly established that a photographer arriving with a camera feels less like an observer than an accomplice.

I decided, on the second day, to photograph only the parts that the postcards leave out. The hour before the concerts. The service corridors. The trams.

## Why black and white

Colour in Vienna is a trap. The city is beige, gold, and grey, and in colour it all collapses into the same warm mud.

HP5 pushed to 800 gave me the contrast to separate the stone from the sky, and the grain to make the emptiness feel deliberate rather than accidental. It is also, frankly, more forgiving in the flat light that Vienna specialises in.

## Medium format discipline

The Rolleiflex gives you twelve frames on a roll and a waist-level finder that shows you the world backwards. Both of these things slow you down, and slowness is the entire point.

I averaged two frames a day. I came home with less than a roll of usable negatives. It is the most satisfied I have been with a set of photographs in years.

## A note on concert photography

I did not photograph inside the Musikverein. It is not permitted, and more importantly it is not appropriate. The photograph of a concert is always worse than the memory of one.

What I photographed instead was the walk home.

## Afterword

Vienna in May is a city of open windows and audible pianos. On the third night I could hear someone practising the slow movement of the Hammerklavier through a courtyard, badly and with great conviction.

I did not photograph it. I stood there for eleven minutes.
