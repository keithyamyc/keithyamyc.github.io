There is a small industry devoted to explaining why this recording is canonical. Most of the explanations are wrong.

The usual account says: Kleiber's tempi are fast, his textures are transparent, his rhythm is incisive. All true, and all insufficient. Plenty of conductors are fast, clear, and rhythmic without producing anything like this.

## What is actually happening

Listen to the first four bars. The famous *da-da-da-DUM* is not played as a motto. It is played as an *event* — a thing that happens, and to which the rest of the movement must respond.

This is the whole of Kleiber's approach in miniature. He does not treat the symphony as a structure to be revealed. He treats it as a sequence of actions.

> The difference between a good Beethoven Fifth and a great one is whether the pauses feel like silence or like held breath.

## The first movement

The tempo is quick but never hurried, which is a distinction that only becomes audible when you hear it done badly (and you will, frequently). What is remarkable is the *balance*: the horns are unusually forward, the woodwinds are placed slightly behind the strings, and the result is a texture with depth rather than width.

The recapitulation — usually the least interesting part of the movement — is genuinely dramatic here. Kleiber slows by perhaps two percent, and the effect is like a door closing.

## The Seventh

The Seventh is the better half, and it is not close.

The first movement's introduction is taken at a genuinely unusual tempo — slower than most, and with an almost military weight. When the *Vivace* arrives it is a release of physical energy that I have not heard equalled.

The *Allegretto* is where most performances die. It is usually played as a slow, mournful procession. Kleiber refuses. His tempo is a real *allegretto* — walking pace — and the effect is far stranger and far more moving. It is not a funeral. It is a crowd moving together, and not knowing why.

## The reissue

This 2023 pressing is cut from a new transfer and is noticeably quieter than the 1990s CD. Surface noise is minimal. The strings have a warmth that the digital versions flatten, and the bass drum in the Seventh's finale has an actual *body*.

It is not a reason to buy a turntable. It is a reason to borrow one.

## Verdict

There are recordings that are historically important and recordings that are simply right. This is both, and it has not been displaced in fifty years.

If you own one Beethoven record, own this one. If you own forty, you already know.

**★★★★★**
