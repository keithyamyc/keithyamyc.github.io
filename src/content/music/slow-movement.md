Concert reviews spend most of their words on the loud parts. The finale gets a paragraph; the scherzo gets a sentence; the slow movement gets an adjective.

This is backwards. If you want to know whether a conductor understands a symphony, listen to the slow movement.

## Why the adagio is the test

Fast music conceals. A certain minimum of energy, precision, and ensemble discipline will carry almost any allegro to an acceptable conclusion. The audience is moved by momentum, and momentum is forgiving.

Slow music has nowhere to hide. Every phrase must be shaped, every balance must be intentional, every silence must be *placed*. An orchestra can bluff its way through a presto. It cannot bluff its way through eight bars of sustained quiet.

> The adagio is where a performance stops being a display and becomes an argument about what the music means.

## Three failures

**The sentimental adagio.** The tempo is slowed until the music becomes a mood rather than a structure. Phrase endings are stretched, dynamics are smoothed, and the result is beautiful and false. This is the most common failure, and the most popular with audiences.

**The impatient adagio.** The conductor, afraid of losing the audience, keeps the music moving at a tempo that prevents it from breathing. Everything is legible, nothing is felt.

**The neutral adagio.** The notes are played correctly, at a sensible tempo, with no view about what they are for. This is the failure of competent conductors, and it is the hardest to criticise, because there is nothing technically wrong.

## Three successes

**Abbado in Mahler's Ninth.** The *adagio* is slow, but the slowness is distributed unevenly: the phrases are long, the transitions are short. The music feels unhurried rather than slow.

**Furtwängler in Schubert's Ninth.** The tempo moves. It breathes in and out across the movement, which by modern standards is deeply incorrect and by any honest standard is completely convincing.

**Wand in Bruckner's Eighth.** Almost metronomic, almost inert on paper, and yet the cumulative effect is overwhelming, because the *balance* is adjusted so precisely that each recurrence of the theme is heard as a new event.

## What to listen for

Next time you are in a concert hall, try this. During the slow movement, stop listening to the melody and listen instead to the inner voices.

- Are the violas audible, or are they filler?
- Does the bass line move independently, or does it merely support?
- When the music pauses, does the silence feel prepared?
- When the theme returns, is it the same theme, or has it been changed by what happened in between?

These questions are answerable in any hall, with any orchestra, for the price of a ticket. They are also, I think, the difference between attending a concert and hearing one.

## Coda

The slow movement is unfashionable because it asks for patience, and patience is difficult to write about. It does not produce the memorable anecdote or the obvious climax.

But it is where the work is done. Everything else is decoration.
