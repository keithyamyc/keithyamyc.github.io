There is a moment near the end of Mahler's Ninth where the music stops being music and becomes something closer to breathing. The strings are marked *ohne Ausdruck* — without expression — and yet the effect, when it works, is the most expressive thing in the symphonic repertoire.

On Saturday it worked. But it took a while to get there.

## The first movement

Van Zweden took the opening faster than I expected. The famous sighing figure in the second violins — the *Lebewohl* motif — was pressed rather than allowed to settle. For the first six or seven minutes I was uneasy. Mahler's first movement is a farewell, and farewells are not usually rushed.

But there was a logic to it. By treating the opening as restless rather than resigned, he made the huge structural crisis in the middle of the movement feel earned rather than inevitable. When the collapse came — and it did come, with the brass in terrifying form — it landed as a genuine event.

> The orchestra played the climax with a control that was almost cruel. You could hear the hall holding its breath.

## The inner movements

The two *Ländler* movements were the weakest part of the evening, and interestingly so. Van Zweden took them at a genuinely rustic tempo, which is defensible, but the HK Phil's brass sounded careful rather than sardonic. Mahler's irony here depends on a kind of sneer that this orchestra does not naturally produce.

The *Rondo-Burleske* was better. The counterpoint was clear, the tempo was fearless, and the trumpet section — which has improved enormously in the last five years — carried the movement's bitterness without overplaying it.

## The adagio

And then the last movement.

I have heard this played faster (Abbado/Berlin, 1999) and slower (Bernstein/Concertgebouw, 1985). Van Zweden chose something in between, but with an unusual emphasis on the *pauses* between phrases. The music was allowed to stop. Repeatedly.

This is risky. In the wrong hands it becomes sentimental — a conductor milking a farewell. Here it felt structural. Each silence was a measure of the distance the music had travelled.

The final page, where the violins repeat the same figure four times, decaying into nothing, was taken at a whisper. The audience did not applaud for a full eight seconds after the last sound. I have never heard the Cultural Centre that quiet.

## Verdict

A Ninth that began as an argument and ended as a farewell. Not a definitive reading — the inner movements are too polite for that — but a serious one, and in the last twenty minutes, a great one.

**★★★★☆**
